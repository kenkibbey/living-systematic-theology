## B1. EXACT CLAIM AND ORIGINAL CONFIDENCE

The question under examination is whether Living Systematic Theology (LST), Version 2.7, adequately accounts for the biblically relevant theological subjects and unresolved questions identified through the independent Candidate A research.

The governing Systematic contains 23 chapters and is dated October 21, 2026.

**Important qualification:** Version 2.7 does not assign itself a separate confidence grade for exhaustive theological completeness. Issue #33 likewise presents the matter as an unresolved examination, not an established finding that the Systematic is either complete or deficient.

The strongest charitable understanding of LST's present position is:

LST seeks a sufficiently comprehensive, coherent, Scripture-governed account of biblical doctrine. It deliberately develops some subjects more extensively than others, distinguishes established teaching from inference, and acknowledges questions that Scripture has not demonstrably settled.

Consequently, an omitted technical term does not automatically establish an omitted doctrine. Nor does a general principle automatically account adequately for every important question beneath it.

The central test is whether the Systematic gives readers a responsible account of the relevant biblical evidence **at the level of completeness its stated purpose requires.**

## B2. STRONGEST AFFIRMATIVE SCRIPTURAL WARRANT FOR RETAINING LST 2.7

The strongest defense is not that the seven disputed subjects are unimportant. It is that **Scripture itself permits theological instruction to organize extensive biblical teaching around foundational truths without reproducing every individual command or disputed application in equal detail.**

### 1. Scripture itself summarizes broader moral obligations

Jesus identifies love for God and neighbor as the commandments upon which the Law and Prophets depend (Matthew 22:37–40). Paul likewise says that particular commandments are summed up in loving one's neighbor (Romans 13:8–10).

These are explicit biblical examples of summary without exhaustive restatement.

LST Chapter 22 follows this recognizable pattern. It grounds Christian conduct in God's character, Christ's teaching, the Spirit's work, love, justice, and practical obedience.

**Direct biblical warrant:** The two great commandments summarize biblical morality.

**Theological inference:** A systematic theology may similarly organize many concrete obligations beneath those governing principles, provided that doing so does not conceal material qualifications or leave crucial questions unaccounted for.

This supports LST's organizational method. It does not automatically prove that Chapter 22 has accounted adequately for the seven disputed subjects.

### 2. The biblical writers themselves employ selective depth

In 1 Corinthians 15:3–4, Paul identifies truths of first importance concerning Christ's death, burial, and resurrection. John 20:30–31 expressly acknowledges that Jesus performed additional signs not recorded in that book and identifies the particular purpose of those selected.

Ephesians 4:11–16 describes the Church's continuing growth through different ministers and gifts rather than a single exhaustive written theological manual.

These passages demonstrate that authoritative instruction can be selective and purpose-driven.

However, the inference must be limited. A Gospel written for a particular purpose is not identical in genre to a systematic theology intended to gather the teaching of the whole canon.

**Affirmative result:** Selective organization is biblically defensible; any specific omission must still be evaluated on its merits.

### 3. LST already contains explicit canonical controls for many disputed applications

This is an important positive strength.

Chapter 19 distinguishes the Mosaic covenant as Israel's historical covenantal administration from the abiding instruction supplied by the entire canon.

Galatians 3:19–25 and Hebrews 8:13 provide genuine biblical reasons not to transfer every Mosaic regulation directly into New Covenant practice. Matthew 5:17–20, Romans 13:8–10, and 2 Timothy 3:16–17 prevent that covenantal transition from becoming permission to discard the Old Testament's moral testimony.

Thus, the Systematic already possesses an interpretive framework relevant to slavery, tithe requirements, debt-release legislation, marital regulation, and civil penalties.

**This is positive support for retaining Chapters 19 and 22 as the appropriate theological framework.** It is not proof that their existing wording supplies the specific conclusions these questions require.

### 4. Scripture permits acknowledged limits to human understanding

Deuteronomy 29:29 distinguishes revealed matters from secret things belonging to God. First Corinthians 13:12 acknowledges the partial character of present knowledge.

LST correctly applies this principle when it openly grades unresolved questions, including aspects of creation, baptism, spiritual gifts, and eschatology.

Still, these passages cannot excuse silence about teachings Scripture actually supplies.

Matthew 28:20 requires disciples to be taught to observe Christ's commands. Second Timothy 3:16–17 describes all Scripture as profitable for teaching and correction.

That creates a significant counterweight: **selective theological depth is permissible, but indefinite neglect of important revealed instructions is not an adequate account of the whole counsel of Scripture.**

### Affirmative warrant assessment

The combined evidence supports three defenses:

- **Strong:** Retaining the 23-chapter canonical architecture instead of adopting Candidate A's sixteen-section structure.
- **Strong:** Preserving existing LST doctrine and confidence grades unless separately challenged by Scripture.
- **Limited:** Treating broad ethical principles as sufficient coverage of every concrete biblical question.

The third defense requires examination of A1–A7 individually. It cannot be established merely from LST's general coherence.

## B3. POINT-BY-POINT RESPONSE TO ADVERSE FINDINGS

### A1 — Marriage, divorce, remarriage, and household exceptions

**Strongest adverse point:** Chapter 22 affirms sexual fidelity but does not address marriage's covenantal structure, Christ's divorce exception language, Paul's instruction about desertion, or remarriage.

**Strongest defense:** Scripture gives positive grounds for deriving marital ethics from larger theological commitments. Genesis 1:27 and 2:24 establish male-and-female creation and one-flesh union. Ephesians 5:22–33 places marital conduct in relation to Christ and His Church. LST already affirms image-bearing humanity, faithfulness, love, holiness, and covenantal responsibility.

Accordingly, the existing architecture has a coherent place for marital doctrine without needing a separate major chapter.

**Concession:** That is a defense of architecture, not an adequate response to the exception texts.

In Matthew 19:3–9, Jesus appeals to creation against divorce for any cause, but Matthew 19:9 contains the exception associated with sexual immorality. The ESV also notes manuscript differences concerning the additional wording of verse 9. Mark 10:11–12 and Luke 16:18 retain especially forceful prohibitions without the same expressed exception.

First Corinthians 7:10–16 distinguishes instructions to married believers from the situation involving an unbelieving spouse who separates. Paul says that the believer is not enslaved in such cases, but the exact implications for subsequent remarriage remain disputed.

The Greek term *porneia* and the relation between Matthew's exception and Paul's instruction warrant actual examination, not an automatic denominational answer.

**Remaining pressure:** The Systematic does not state what its marital obligations mean when confronted with these passages. Its general requirement of fidelity cannot settle the disputed cases.

**Defense disposition:** A1 succeeds against complete explicit coverage. It does not establish that LST's existing moral principles are erroneous or that Candidate A's particular conclusions should be adopted.

**Defensible response:** Preserve the architecture; add an accountable treatment of marriage, divorce, exceptions, separation, and remarriage, with unresolved applications separately identified.

### A2 — Slavery, servitude, man-stealing, and emancipation

**Strongest adverse point:** LST condemns exploitation and confesses human dignity but does not explicitly confront the biblical institution of slavery, including its difficult permissions and restrictions.

**Strongest defense:** Genesis 1:26–27, Genesis 9:6, and James 3:9 establish the dignity of persons made in God's image. Ephesians 2:11–22 and Galatians 3:28 establish reconciliation and equal standing in Christ.

LST Chapter 5 explicitly rejects exploitation and contempt for vulnerable persons. Chapter 20 confesses one reconciled people in Christ, while Chapter 22 demands impartial justice and care for the vulnerable.

These provide substantive moral principles rather than mere empty generalities.

Exodus 21:16 and 1 Timothy 1:10 also directly condemn man-stealing or enslavement through kidnapping.

**Concession:** These texts do not remove the problem presented by Leviticus 25:44–46, which permits permanent possession of non-Israelite slaves, or by New Testament household instructions such as Ephesians 6:5–9.

The defense cannot simply classify the Mosaic regulations as temporary and announce that the ethical problem has disappeared. Scripture's actual accommodation and regulation must first be stated truthfully.

Nor does Philemon explicitly contain a universal legislative command abolishing slavery. Yet Paul's appeal that Onesimus be received as a beloved brother, and as Paul himself would be received, places remarkable pressure on ordinary master-slave relationships.

**Remaining pressure:** The difficult texts involve legal permissions, human ownership, unequal treatment, apostolic household instructions, and the moral implications of Christian brotherhood. None is adequately resolved by the general prohibition against exploitation.

**Defense disposition:** A2 succeeds as a substantial coverage criticism. A particular theory of how Scripture moves from ancient regulation toward emancipation remains a separate exegetical question.

**Defensible response:** Retain the image-of-God and New Covenant framework, but explicitly acknowledge the regulations, prohibitions, hard permissions, and transformative implications.

### A3 — Economic ethics, wealth, debt, generosity, and tithing

**Strongest adverse point:** Chapter 22 contains economic ethics, but it does not develop biblical teaching about debt, wealth, tithing, or the continuity of Israel's economic institutions.

**Strongest defense:** This is not an absence comparable to A2.

LST explicitly addresses just wages, employment integrity, generosity, poverty, injustice, and Israel's land-and-release responsibilities under Leviticus 25.

Its general approach has substantial biblical support in James 5:1–6, Leviticus 19, Deuteronomy 24, and the prophetic condemnations of economic oppression.

Second Corinthians 8–9 reinforces willing generosity; Matthew 6:19–24 warns against serving wealth rather than God; and 1 Timothy 6:17–19 addresses wealthy believers without treating ownership itself as sinful.

Much of the central moral doctrine is therefore already represented.

**Concession:** The existing coverage does not distinguish Israel's statutory tithe requirements from New Covenant giving, nor does it offer a developed account of debt, lending, the danger of riches, or economic restitution.

Malachi 3:8–10 addresses covenantal obligations in Israel. Transferring its tithe requirements directly into a universal obligation upon Christians requires positive New Covenant justification.

Likewise, the debt-release regulations must be interpreted in their covenantal setting without declaring their enduring ethical significance irrelevant.

One factual refinement to A3: the term *debt* occurs in LST's discussion of a parable, but not as a developed economic subject.

**Remaining pressure:** The absence of specific mechanisms could permit readers to assume that LST's general generosity language establishes positions it has not actually defended.

**Defense disposition:** A3 partly succeeds. Considerable affirmative economic coverage already exists, but meaningful applications remain unaccounted for.

**Defensible response:** A concise expansion that separates demonstrated economic obligations from contested covenantal applications should suffice; a separate comprehensive economic theology is not yet established as necessary.

### A4 — Prenatal life and difficult human-life ethics

**Strongest adverse point:** Chapters 5 and 22 affirm intrinsic human dignity and protection of life but do not discuss prenatal persons or the passages used to determine their moral status.

**Strongest defense:** The image of God is already fundamental to LST anthropology. Genesis 1:26–27 identifies humanity as image-bearing, and Genesis 9:6 grounds protection against bloodshed in that status.

Psalm 139:13–16 portrays God's knowledge and formation of a person in the womb. Luke 1:41–44 likewise identifies a child living before birth.

These passages offer substantial positive biblical grounds for extending LST's existing human-dignity framework to prenatal life.

**Concession:** The specific move from human dignity in general to particular prenatal moral and legal conclusions is not expressly developed in LST.

Psalm 139 is personal, poetic praise, not a technical biological account. Luke 1 contains a remarkable prenatal narrative but does not alone specify every disputed moral threshold.

Exodus 21:22–25 is particularly important because English versions differ in their rendering of the injury involving a pregnant woman. The NIV describes premature birth; the ESV refers to her children coming out. The relation between the initial event, subsequent harm, and legal penalties requires careful investigation.

One must not pretend that translating Exodus 21 a particular way settles the whole prenatal question.

**Remaining pressure:** General image-bearing doctrine does not substitute for an explicit account of prenatal dignity, wrongful killing, difficult cases, and the limits of biblical legal conclusions.

**Defense disposition:** A4 succeeds against developed topical coverage while leaving LST's foundational anthropology substantially intact.

**Defensible response:** A short, text-governed subsection could connect Chapters 5 and 22 without importing Candidate A's separate grades.

### A5 — Human constitution: soul, spirit, and body

**Strongest adverse point:** Chapter 5 addresses humanity's creation, dignity, faculties, and fall but not the disputed constitution of the person.

**Strongest defense:** This objection must consider Chapter 23.

LST already makes substantive claims about bodily resurrection, conscious fellowship with Christ after death, and the intermediate state. Second Corinthians 5:1–8 and Philippians 1:21–24 are directly interpreted there.

Therefore, the Systematic does not neglect personal identity after death or bodily resurrection.

Nor does Genesis 2:7 require adoption of a developed dichotomous or trichotomous ontology simply because it describes the formation of the living human being.

First Thessalonians 5:23 speaks of spirit, soul, and body in Paul's prayer for complete sanctification. Hebrews 4:12 speaks of the Word's penetrating division of soul and spirit. A serious argument exists that these are descriptions of the entire human person and divine penetration into the inward life, rather than a fixed inventory of separable components. Competing readings nevertheless remain recognizable and should not be dismissed as linguistically impossible.

**Concession:** Neither Chapter 5 nor Chapter 23 explicitly addresses the interpretive dispute surrounding dichotomy, trichotomy, and the origin of the soul.

The original adverse finding must therefore be narrowed, not dismissed. LST covers significant anthropological and eschatological conclusions, but not the disputed compositional framework.

Also, Candidate A itself identifies the soul's origin as outside the completed scope of its T2-14 examination. Its greater topical detail cannot be treated as exhaustive treatment of that question.

**Remaining pressure:** A reader cannot determine whether LST deliberately refuses a compositional model or has simply not examined the question.

**Defense disposition:** A5 partly succeeds. The doctrine of embodied resurrection and intermediate personal existence is already developed, while constitution and origin remain insufficiently explicit.

**Defensible response:** A short open-status note in Chapter 5, linked to Chapter 23, would likely satisfy the demonstrated coverage need.

### A6 — Fasting, anointing, laying on of hands, and foot washing

**Strongest adverse point:** Scripture directly instructs or describes practices not individually addressed in LST's discussions of prayer, church life, and ordinances.

**Strongest defense:** Not every New Testament practice automatically becomes an ordinance equivalent to baptism or the Lord's Supper.

Acts 13:2–3 narrates fasting, prayer, and the laying on of hands when workers are sent. Narrative occurrence establishes that the practice took place, not necessarily that every congregation must reproduce every historical circumstance.

John 13:12–17 expressly commands disciples to follow Jesus' example of humble service. Whether this establishes a permanently binding ritual form of foot washing requires further argument.

LST's existing distinction between fundamental ordinances and the wider Christian life is therefore defensible.

**Concession:** James 5:14–15 is not merely an incidental historical narrative. James instructs a sick person to call the church's elders for prayer and anointing with oil in the Lord's name. This is more specific than Chapter 22's general affirmation of prayer.

Jesus' teaching in Matthew 6:16–18 likewise assumes fasting and regulates its sincerity.

These passages warrant acknowledgment without concluding prematurely that anointing is mechanically effective, that healing is invariably received, or that foot washing constitutes a universally required third ordinance.

**Remaining pressure:** LST gives substantial consideration to disputed charismatic gifts in Chapter 8 while offering no comparable direct acknowledgment of James 5's pastoral instruction.

**Defense disposition:** A6 succeeds as a coverage criticism, especially concerning prayer and anointing for the sick. It does not establish that LST's ordinance classification is mistaken.

**Defensible response:** A compact paragraph identifying the practices, their biblical status, and their unresolved questions is more defensible than adding new ordinances without sufficient proof.

### A7 — Civil government, the sword, and Christian responsibility

**Strongest adverse point:** LST speaks about justice and prayer for rulers but does not explain governmental authority, the sword, or the limits of Christian submission.

**Strongest defense:** Chapters 4, 19, 20, and 22 jointly establish divine sovereignty, covenantal distinctions, the Church's noncoercive mission, justice, peacemaking, and prayer for rulers.

The prohibition of personal vengeance in Romans 12:17–21 fits that larger approach.

Romans 13:1–7 then explicitly describes governing authorities as established by God and connects their authority with public order, punishment, taxation, and the sword.

Acts 5:29 establishes an important limit: obedience to God takes precedence when authorities command disobedience to Him.

**Concession:** These latter texts are not adequately accounted for in LST's present exposition.

Romans 12 and 13 must be read together: Paul's prohibition against personal vengeance is followed by his account of public authority's punitive function. Neither passage alone settles all questions about war, capital punishment, civil service, or revolution.

Likewise, Acts 5:29 does not license every form of resistance merely because a believer considers government unjust.

**Remaining pressure:** A systematic that addresses God's providence over history and the Church's public mission still needs to distinguish the Church's own calling from the state's authority and the limits imposed by God's superior claim.

**Defense disposition:** A7 succeeds as a meaningful coverage criticism but does not establish that LST must adopt Candidate A's particular political conclusions.

**Defensible response:** Retain the framework and add a focused treatment of authority, submission, the sword, conscientious disobedience, and unresolved applications.

## B4. SELF-CROSS-EXAMINATION

### Would this defense stand if LST had never taught these things?

**For the architecture, yes.** The biblical witness supports organizing doctrine around God, creation, Christ, redemption, covenant, Church, obedience, judgment, and consummation.

There is no biblical requirement for a systematic theology to use Candidate A's section divisions, produce a separate chapter for every theological controversy, or reproduce every source dossier.

**For the present level of coverage on all seven subjects, no equally strong defense has been established.**

A theology that confesses general moral principles does not thereby prove it has adequately accounted for the biblical passages that specify those principles.

The distinction matters especially when an author's particular instruction presents a question that is absent from the general statement.

Examples include Matthew's exception language, Paul's instructions concerning unbelieving spouses, the legal treatment of slavery, James's pastoral instruction concerning anointing, and Romans 13's treatment of the sword.

These are not merely imagined theological controversies. They are questions generated by biblical passages themselves.

### Could the defender be applying unequal standards?

Yes. This is the most serious self-directed criticism.

LST spends substantial space on difficult questions involving baptism, Romans 11, Revelation 20, and the warnings in Hebrews. Those extended discussions show that the system recognizes the value of openly recording opposing texts and qualified conclusions.

It would be inconsistent to defend that standard in those chapters while categorically dismissing similarly concrete difficulties in ethics as unnecessary details.

There is a possible distinction: LST has developed the controversies most structurally important to its doctrinal architecture. But that is an editorial explanation, not yet positive biblical proof that the other subjects need no explicit acknowledgment.

### Could Candidate A be setting an artificially expansive standard?

Yes.

Its broader topical inventory does not establish that every subject merits equal length, a new doctrinal conclusion, or a new chapter.

Moreover, several Candidate A dossiers explicitly acknowledge limited original-language study, bounded research rounds, missing historical sources, or unresolved interpretive questions.

It would be mistaken to turn the completeness examination into wholesale acceptance of Candidate A's conclusions.

### What would make the defense abandon its position?

The defense should abandon the claim that existing general coverage is adequate wherever the following can be demonstrated:

1. Scripture addresses a significant subject directly.
2. The present Systematic neither treats the relevant instructions nor provides a responsible route or open-status acknowledgment.
3. The omission conceals a material distinction, exception, command, or interpretive difficulty.
4. Adding the subject would improve theological accountability without introducing an unsupported doctrinal conclusion.

Conversely, a proposed addition should be rejected as redundant if existing text already answers the relevant biblical question with sufficient clarity and depth.

**Confidence warning:** No confession-level or defended-synthesis grade for overall completeness exists in LST 2.7. If someone asserted that every material biblical subject is already adequately addressed, the present evidence would not sustain that assertion.

### Hidden assumptions requiring explicit testing

The judge should examine whether the two sides have quietly assumed that:

- A comprehensive systematic theology must treat every important biblical subject at the same depth.
- A general moral principle necessarily covers all its difficult applications.
- An absent term proves an absent doctrine.
- Mentioning a verse constitutes meaningful engagement with its argument.
- A question identified by Candidate A is automatically a defect in LST.
- Biblical relevance requires a settled theological conclusion rather than a qualified or open acknowledgment.
- A brief addition necessarily provides sufficient coverage rather than simply creating the appearance of completeness.

### Questions that could change the result

**1. What is the proper minimum standard of completeness?**

Would a traceable open-status paragraph satisfy Issue #33's stated standard for accounting for a question, or do certain subjects require developed exegesis because their biblical importance and competing interpretations demand more?

**2. Which subjects contain direct instructions that LST's existing categories cannot fairly substitute for?**

James 5:14 and Romans 13:4 provide especially useful tests because their instructions are concrete and their terms cannot be supplied merely by invoking prayer or justice.

**3. Should a coverage addition state biblical observations without asserting a new doctrine?**

The judge should distinguish a note such as Scripture directly addresses the following question from a doctrinal conclusion that resolves the question. Only the latter requires the full evidentiary burden for a proposed new theological position.

**4. Are any requested expansions based primarily on Candidate A's editorial preferences rather than Scripture?**

The 16-section Candidate A structure and its dossier grading scheme are not independent biblical evidence. The relevant question is whether its inventory reveals genuine biblical subject matter that LST has failed to account for.

**5. Could a carefully organized cross-reference accomplish the same task with fewer changes?**

This question is especially important for A3 and A5, which already have substantial corresponding material elsewhere in LST.

**6. Does the examination need to return to the other fourteen subject groups?**

Yes, before Issue #33's complete 21-group coverage matrix can be considered examined. The seven findings presented here are not evidence that the remaining fourteen have received a full independent defense or verification.

### Verification limits for the judge

The present defense directly inspected LST's governing text and Candidate A's compiled synthesis, index, and methods disclosure. It also considered selected biblical passages in published English translations, including identifiable translation differences and the ESV's manuscript note at Matthew 19:9.

It did **not** independently collate Hebrew or Greek manuscript witnesses, examine a complete critical apparatus, or verify all original-language assertions and source records within Candidate A's unpublished detailed dossiers. The accessible PDF's text was extracted through the connected Drive source; the public web reader did not provide a separate visual page verification.

The judge should require additional original-language and historical investigation where those questions could materially determine an actual doctrinal formulation, especially divorce exceptions, Exodus 21:22–25, and the slavery regulations.

## DEFENDER'S CLOSING SUBMISSION

The strongest defensible position is that LST Version 2.7 has a substantial, biblically grounded canonical architecture that should not be discarded merely because independent research identifies additional theological questions.

Its general doctrines of human dignity, justice, Christian obedience, covenantal development, and Church life already provide meaningful foundations for the seven areas examined.

But those foundations should not be mistaken for complete explicit treatment. Several of the adverse findings identify significant biblical instructions that the present text does not adequately bring into view.

**The affirmative case supports retaining LST's architecture and biblical principles while remaining open to proportionate correction of its coverage. It does not support treating Candidate A as doctrinal authority, importing its conclusions, or defending silence for the sake of preserving Version 2.7 unchanged.**

This completes the assigned **B1–B6 Revision Resistance defense**. The individual attack findings and the remaining positive case have been stated separately.

**No neutral final ruling is supplied. No governing LST files have been changed by this examination. Independent adjudication (Claude, Role C) and verification (Gemini, Role D) remain outstanding.**
