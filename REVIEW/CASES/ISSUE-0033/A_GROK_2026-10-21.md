**SECTION B — STRONGEST ADVERSE FINDINGS**

**A1 — Marriage, divorce, remarriage, and household exceptions**  
Exact relevant LST 2.7 wording (Chapter 22): “The moral life is concrete. Scripture requires truthful speech, sexual fidelity, protection of human life, integrity in employment, just wages, generosity…” No further development of marital dissolution, exceptions, or remarriage appears anywhere in the document (terms “divorce,” “remarriage” absent on full-text search).  
Corresponding SFT: §14 T2-37/38, T3-24.  
Biblical relevance: Genesis 2:24 (one-flesh permanence); Matthew 5:31–32 and 19:3–9 (exception clause “except for sexual immorality”); 1 Corinthians 7:10–16 (abandonment by unbeliever; “not enslaved”); Deuteronomy 24:1–4 (historical regulation); Ephesians 5:22–33 and 1 Peter 3:1–7 (household duties). These are concrete, repeated apostolic and dominical instructions governing the most basic human relation, not peripheral.  
Why insufficient: Chapter 22 states the general obligation of sexual fidelity and one-flesh ideal but supplies no local exegesis, exception analysis, or open-question marker for the texts that explicitly qualify permanence. A systematic ethics chapter that lists “sexual fidelity” while remaining silent on the New Testament’s own exception language leaves a central household question unaccounted for.  
Strongest defense of existing coverage: The general commands of fidelity, love of neighbor, and protection of the vulnerable (Chapter 22) imply restraint; household order is presupposed in broader obedience material (Chapters 19–20).  
Smallest justified correction: A short subsection or explicit open-status pointer in Chapter 22 that quotes the controlling texts, notes the exception language, records the interpretive pressures (e.g., scope of *porneia*, relation of 1 Cor 7:15 to Matt 19), and states what is demonstrated versus underdetermined.  
Confidence in finding: High (absence confirmed by full-text search).  
What might overturn: Demonstration that the exception texts are treated under a different heading with sustained local meaning analysis.

**A2 — Slavery, servitude, man-stealing, and emancipation**  
Exact relevant LST 2.7 wording: None. Terms “slavery,” “slave,” “servitude” absent on full-text search. Chapter 22 lists general justice and care for the vulnerable but contains no discussion of the household codes or Mosaic regulations.  
Corresponding SFT: §14 T2-42.  
Biblical relevance: Exodus 21; Leviticus 25; Deuteronomy 15, 23; Ephesians 6:5–9; Colossians 3:22–4:1; 1 Peter 2:18–21; Philemon; 1 Timothy 1:10 (man-stealing condemned). These texts regulate an institution present in both Testaments and address its ethical boundaries, redemption, and transformation in Christ.  
Why insufficient: A Christian ethics chapter that addresses “just wages,” “care for… the poor,” and “no exploitation” while remaining silent on the explicit biblical treatment of servitude leaves a major historical and moral topic without systematic account. The texts are neither peripheral nor reducible to generic justice.  
Strongest defense: Broader prohibitions of injustice and the equality of believers in Christ (implied in Chapters 14, 20, 22) supply the moral direction; the topic may be viewed as historically contingent.  
Smallest justified correction: An explicit treatment or open-question note in Chapter 22 that records the Mosaic regulations, the household codes, the condemnation of man-stealing, and the implications of union in Christ, distinguishing what is regulated from what is commanded as perpetual.  
Confidence: High.  
What might overturn: Location of sustained treatment under another heading that engages the primary texts.

**A3 — Economic ethics: wealth, poverty, debt, generosity, and tithing**  
Exact relevant LST 2.7 wording (Chapter 22): “integrity in employment, just wages, generosity… care for widows, orphans, strangers, and the poor”; brief appeal to Leviticus 25 on land and the vulnerable. Terms “tithe,” “tithing,” “debt” absent.  
Corresponding SFT: §14 T2-40.  
Biblical relevance: Proverbs on wealth and poverty; Malachi 3:8–10; Matthew 6:19–34; 2 Corinthians 8–9; 1 Timothy 6:6–19; James 5:1–6; Old Testament tithe and debt-release laws. These form a sustained biblical concern with stewardship, not a single verse.  
Why insufficient: General affirmations of generosity and justice are present, yet the specific biblical mechanisms and debates (tithing continuity, debt release, wealth as idol) receive no local development. The chapter’s own citation of Leviticus 25 invites further ethical unpacking that is not supplied.  
Strongest defense: The listed obligations and the land/labor paragraph already express the moral substance; detailed casuistry is unnecessary for systematic coherence.  
Smallest justified correction: A brief subsection or pointer that inventories the principal texts, notes what is demonstrated (generosity, care for the poor, warning against idolatry of wealth), and records what remains underdetermined (exact continuity of the tithe).  
Confidence: Moderate-to-high.  
What might overturn: Showing that the Leviticus 25 reference and surrounding commands already constitute adequate systematic account.

**A4 — Human life: prenatal life and difficult life-ethics questions**  
Exact relevant LST 2.7 wording (Chapter 5): “Human life therefore possesses an intrinsic dignity not conferred by status, strength, usefulness, or government (Genesis 9:6; James 3:9).” Chapter 22: “protection of human life.” Terms “unborn,” “abortion,” “prenatal” absent.  
Corresponding SFT: §14 T2-41.  
Biblical relevance: Genesis 1:26–27; 9:6; Psalm 139:13–16; Exodus 21:22–25; Luke 1:41–44. The image-bearing status and protection of life are foundational; the prenatal texts raise concrete questions about vulnerability and intentional destruction.  
Why insufficient: Intrinsic dignity and general protection are confessed, yet the specific biblical materials that address life in the womb and the hard cases receive no engagement. A systematic anthropology and ethics that affirm the image while remaining silent on the texts most frequently cited for prenatal status leaves a material question unaccounted for.  
Strongest defense: The image-of-God confession and the general prohibition of murder already settle the moral direction; further casuistry is pastoral rather than systematic.  
Smallest justified correction: An explicit note in Chapter 5 or 22 that cites the principal texts, states what they establish about the status of the unborn, and records remaining difficulties (e.g., precise legal applications).  
Confidence: High.  
What might overturn: Demonstration of sustained treatment elsewhere that engages Psalm 139 and Exodus 21.

Corresponding SFT: §5 T2-14, T3-04.  
Biblical relevance: Genesis 2:7; 1 Thessalonians 5:23; Hebrews 4:12; Ecclesiastes 12:7; competing readings of body-soul relation and postmortem identity. These texts are foundational for anthropology and eschatology.

Strongest defense: The image-bearing person is treated as a unitary agent; compositional theories are secondary and underdetermined.  
Smallest justified correction: A short open-status paragraph in Chapter 5 that records the principal texts and states that dichotomy, trichotomy, and traducianism/creationism remain underdetermined.  
Confidence: Moderate-to-high.  
What might overturn: Showing that the unitary-person treatment already satisfies the systematic need.

**SECTION D — HANDOFF**

Strongest coverage deficiencies established: A1 (marriage/divorce/remarriage), A2 (slavery/servitude), A4 (prenatal life ethics), A6 (named worship practices), and A7 (civil government/sword). These rest on confirmed absence of key terms and lack of local engagement with controlling texts inside the relevant chapters.

Precise current LST wording the defender must account for: Chapter 22’s list of concrete moral obligations (“sexual fidelity, protection of human life… generosity… care for… the poor”) and its brief Leviticus 25 paragraph; Chapter 5’s image-dignity confession; the absence of the named topics on full-text search.

Strongest biblical reasons for expansion: The New Testament itself supplies exception language for marriage (Matt 19; 1 Cor 7), household codes for servitude (Eph 6; Philemon), explicit prenatal language (Ps 139; Ex 21), commanded or exemplified practices (Jas 5:14; Matt 6:16), and a direct authorization of the sword (Rom 13). A systematic ethics and anthropology that affirms the general duties while remaining silent on the texts that qualify or instantiate them leaves those questions unaccounted for.

Best reasons for retaining existing structure: The 23-chapter architecture already supplies a coherent canonical frame; broad ethical obligations and the image confession imply moral direction; not every specialized question requires a settled paragraph; Candidate A’s greater length does not automatically prove superior completeness; targeted open-status notes preserve focus without importing external conclusions.

Evidence needed to distinguish genuine omissions from redundant additions: Side-by-side demonstration that the controlling passages receive sustained local-meaning analysis under an existing heading, or a clear statement that the question is deliberately deferred with the biblical pressure recorded.
