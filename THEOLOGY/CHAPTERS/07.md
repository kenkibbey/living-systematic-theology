<h2 align="center">The Atonement: Accomplishment, Extent, and Proclamation</h2>
<p align="center"><em>Chapter 7 · LST 2.9 · <a href="../../LIVING_SYSTEMATIC_THEOLOGY.md">Complete authoritative Systematic</a></em></p>
<p align="center">✦ Scripture governs · Every claim remains testable ✦</p>

The death of Christ is penal, substitutionary, priestly, propitiatory, reconciling, redemptive, covenantal, and victorious. These are descriptions of one saving work, not rival accomplishments. The Servant bears sin and brings justification (Isaiah 53). God displays His justice in justifying sinners through Christ's blood (Romans 3:21-26). Christ becomes a curse for His people (Galatians 3:13). He obtains eternal redemption and perfects those sanctified by one offering (Hebrews 9:12; 10:14). His blood purchases a people from every tribe, language, people, and nation (Revelation 5:9). Christ's saving work is not merely an unrealized possibility: He actually saves those whom the Father gives Him. That claim must not be turned into a rule that every Pauline use of "Christ died for" by itself proves that the person so described is already secured, because Romans 14:15 and 1 Corinthians 8:11 apply that language to a brother whose destruction is seriously warned against.

The priestly offering is final. Animals could not take away sin or perfect the conscience. Christ offered Himself once and sat down. Where forgiveness is given, no further offering for sin remains (Hebrews 9:11-28; 10:1-18). His intercession does not repeat the sacrifice. The risen Priest lives to bring the finished work to its appointed end in those who draw near through Him (Hebrews 7:25-27; Romans 8:34).

Four claims must be kept distinct. The incarnate Son's offering lacks no intrinsic worth. LST calls this unlimited sufficiency: no further sacrifice would be required had God purposed to save more. The command to preach Christ runs to every person. The invitation is sincere. Anyone who comes will not be cast out (John 6:37; Acts 17:30; Romans 10:13). The Father, Son, and Spirit save particular people. Election, giving, shepherding, church-purchase, and effectual intercession name that purpose. Those whom He thus saves He infallibly brings to glory. Accomplishment at the cross and application in history are not two rival atonements. The elect remain children of wrath until union with Christ (Ephesians 2:3). Delay is not failure.

Sufficiency and universal proclamation do not, by themselves, prove or disprove particular saving intention. Particular texts do not, by themselves, empty every universal word. Each sentence must do the work its author gives it.

The particular passages are not ambiguous about purpose. The Shepherd lays down His life for the sheep; they hear, follow, and will never perish; He does not speak in this way of those who are not His sheep (John 10:11, 15, 26-29). He loves the Church and gives Himself for her to make her holy (Ephesians 5:25-27). God purchased the Church with His own blood (Acts 20:28). He saves His people from their sins (Matthew 1:21). Romans 8:32-34 joins the giving-up of the Son, the granting of all things, justification, and intercession for the elect. These texts teach an effective particular saving purpose. Particular inclusion and exclusive extent are nevertheless distinct claims. John 10 establishes that Christ dies for His sheep and certainly saves them. It does not, by itself, establish that He dies for nobody else. Whether the particular texts also forbid every wider substitutionary reference is a further question. It is not settled by repeating "sheep."

The universal-sounding texts must be heard at full volume.

Hebrews 2:9 says He tasted death for everyone. The paragraph stands under Psalm 8's human vocation and then names many sons, brothers, children given to the Son, and Abraham's offspring (2:5-18). That family is the letter's saving company. Verse 9 still says everyone, and it does not add "only the sons." The scope is exegetical. It is not decided by a preferred inflection or by the prior truth of election.

1 Timothy 2:1-7 commands prayer for all people, including kings; says God desires all to be saved; names one God and one Mediator; and says He gave Himself as a ransom for all. LST reads the paragraph as refusing every exclusion of rank, class, or nation, especially those the church would neglect. That reading is strong. A reference to every individual is also a serious reading of the repeated "all," the common humanity of verse 5, and the movement from prayer to ransom. "Kings" does not automatically shrink every later "all" to "kinds." LST's particular-intent conclusion is a canonical synthesis. This paragraph does not itself say "not every person."

1 John 2:2 calls Christ the propitiation for our sins, and not for ours only but also for the whole world. The sentence states a scope: not the writer's circle only, but the world. LST must distinguish that stated scope from the application of the work's benefits. The verse does not teach that every individual is finally saved. It also does not arrive with a restricted referent already inserted. John 11:51-52 contrasts one nation with the scattered children of God, but that text names the children; 1 John 2:2 names the world. The children cannot simply be placed into this sentence. What "propitiation" does in John's argument must be shown from John, not from a rule that the noun can attach only to the finally saved.

John 1:29 announces the Lamb who takes away the sin of the world. The claim is the unique and cosmic sufficiency of His work. It does not supply a roster of each person whose guilt is removed.

2 Peter 2:1 speaks of false teachers who deny the Master who bought them and who face destruction. Covenantal ownership of visible-community members is a possible account. It must be shown from Peter's purchase language and setting. Their ruin does not prove the reading by itself. A broader purchase reading must then say in what sense the bought perish.

1 Timothy 4:10 calls God the Savior of all people, especially of believers. Providential preservation and differentiated saving action are proposed senses. The verse does not define the extent of penal substitution.

2 Corinthians 5:14-21 says one died for all, therefore all died; God was reconciling the world in Christ; and the apostles appeal, "be reconciled to God." Romans 5:12-21 sets Adam and Christ in representative parallel. Romans 5:17-19 must be read as a complete argument: those who receive the abundance of grace and the gift of righteousness reign in life; then Paul speaks of justification and of the many made righteous. The meaning of "all" and "the many" is established from that movement, not merely from the fact of an Adam-Christ parallel. LST may not refuse a wider sense only because it distinguishes accomplishment from application. A wider sense must not empty the verbs for those who remain condemned.

Romans 14:15 and 1 Corinthians 8:11 belong in the extent file. Paul tells the strong not to destroy "the one for whom Christ died" and says that by the strong person's knowledge "the brother for whom Christ died" is destroyed. The phrase uses Paul's ordinary language of Christ's death for persons, and LST may not redefine brother or died for in advance to protect a particular-intent synthesis. The verb for destroy can bear eschatological force in Paul, and 1 Corinthians 8-10 places the warning beside Israel's destruction, idolatry, and the command to take heed lest one fall. Romans 14:4, however, says of the servant under dispute that he will be upheld because the Lord is able to make him stand. These texts therefore create real pressure without themselves stating either that a savingly united person finally perishes or that Christ bore every individual's sins with the same saving intention. Most importantly for the extent question, Paul's words show that "Christ died for this person" cannot by itself function as proof that the person is secured. The precise relation of this Pauline usage to the extent of saving sin-bearing must be argued from the whole atonement file rather than settled by the preposition or formula alone.

The synthesis LST defends is therefore this: Christ's offering is infinitely sufficient; the gospel is universally commanded and sincerely promised; His saving sin-bearing and intercession secure the people given Him by the Father. This synthesis does not claim that every occurrence of "Christ died for" has, by itself, the technical sense "a person infallibly secured by the atonement." That third claim is gathered from the unity of the particular texts with election and effectual calling. It is not the dictionary meaning of every "world." If a passage were shown to teach a substitutionary saving intention for every individual as such, the extent thesis would have to be revised. The finished efficacy of the cross for all who are in Christ would not be surrendered with it.

The preacher does not need the hidden decree in order to preach. Christ is the only Savior. All are commanded to repent and believe. Everyone who comes is received. No sinner is turned away because the sacrifice lacked worth. Refusal is the hearer's own guilt. Sovereign grace is a reason to pray and to go, not a reason to withhold the word (Romans 10:1, 14-17; 2 Timothy 2:10).
